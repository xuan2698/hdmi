The reset and power-up behavior in this specification is deliberately underspecified, and that is the most important thing for the final checker to respect. The trace begins with `clk=0`, `a=1`, and `b=x`, with `state` and `q` both unknown. At 5 ns the clock rises while `b` is still unknown, and the state remains unknown. Only at 10 ns does `b` become a known 0, but the state is still unknown at that row. The first row in which the state is known is 15 ns, where `state=0` and `q=0`. This means the circuit has no observable reset value in the public trace: the initial stored bit is simply unknown until the first settled known-state row. A checker must therefore not assume a particular power-up value, nor require that the first sampled state equal zero. It should treat the 15 ns row as the first trustworthy observation of the memory element, not as evidence about reset.

Equally important, the rows at 0–10 ns must not be used as transition sources. The 5 ns row has `b=x`, and the 10 ns row has unknown state. Any transition inferred from those rows would be based on unknown values and could falsely constrain the implementation. The checker should skip any consecutive pair where the earlier row’s state is unknown or where either row’s inputs are unknown. In this trace that means the first usable transition is from the 15 ns row (state 0) to the 20 ns row (inputs 0,0, next state 0). The 15 ns row itself is usable as a source because its state is known and the 20 ns row’s inputs are known; the unknown inputs at 0–10 ns do not contaminate it.

The prose’s parity formula is a trap. It fits some early transitions, but the trace contains a direct counterexample. From 70 ns (state 0) to 75 ns (inputs 0,0), the observed next state is 1, while parity predicts 0. From 60 ns (state 0) to 65 ns (inputs 1,1), the observed next state is 0, which parity also predicts. Those two entries together show that the next-state function is not `state XOR a XOR b`. A checker that encodes the parity rule would reject the public trace at the 75 ns transition. The correct approach is to derive the next-state table from consecutive settled rows, keyed by `(prev_state, a, b)`, skipping unknown rows, and then verify consistency: every usable transition must match the table, and no key may map to two different next states. This accepts the public waveform without trusting an incomplete verbal rule, and it catches implementations that merely fit the first few rows.

A short worked example makes the point concrete. The transition from 70 ns to 75 ns gives the mapping `(0,0,0) -> 1`. The transition from 60 ns to 65 ns gives `(0,1,1) -> 0`. Those two entries alone disprove the simple parity machine, so the checker should derive behavior from the trace rather than encode the prose formula. The checker should also avoid assuming any reset value, since the trace never shows a known state before 15 ns.
