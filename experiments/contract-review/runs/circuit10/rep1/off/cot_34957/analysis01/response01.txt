The public trace is a single-bit sequential machine sampled on the rising edge of `clk`. The columns `a` and `b` are the inputs; `state` is the observable flip-flop output; `q` is the circuit output. The key timing convention is that each row lists the values present before the sampled edge, and any mid-cycle input change occurs after sampling and is carried into the next row. Thus the meaningful transition is from the `state` shown in one row to the `state` shown in the next row, using the `a,b` values in the earlier row at the rising edge.

Looking at the trace, the first informative sampled edge is at 15 ns: before it, `a=0,b=0`, and the prior state is unknown, but after the edge `state=0` and `q=0`. From then on, the state changes only on certain input combinations. The clearest pattern is that `state` becomes 1 when `a=0,b=1` at a rising edge: at 45 ns the inputs are `0,1`, and the next state shown at 50 ns is 1. Similarly, at 75 ns the inputs are `0,0` but the state was already 1 from the previous edge, and at 85 ns the inputs `1,1` drive the next state to 0. At 95 ns the inputs are `1,1` and the next state is 1, which appears to contradict a simple “set on 01, clear on 11” reading unless the state at 95 ns is being interpreted from the previous row.

The important subtlety is that the `state` column in a row is the flip-flop value *after* the edge for that row, while `q` is the combinational output for the same row. For example, at 45 ns the row shows `a=0,b=1,state=0,q=1`; the `q=1` is combinational from the current inputs and current state, while the next state at 50 ns is 1. At 65 ns the row shows `a=1,b=1,state=0,q=0`; the next state at 70 ns is 0. At 75 ns the row shows `a=0,b=0,state=1,q=1`; the next state at 80 ns is 1. At 85 ns the row shows `a=1,b=1,state=0,q=0`; the next state at 90 ns is 0. At 95 ns the row shows `a=1,b=1,state=1,q=1`; the next state at 100 ns is 1. This suggests the next-state function is not simply a function of `a,b` alone; it depends on the current state as well.

A useful point for the final checker is to align each transition with the row whose inputs are sampled at the rising edge, not with the row where the new state first appears. The row at time `t` gives the inputs just before the edge at `t`, and the `state` at `t` is the post-edge value. Therefore the transition from `state(t)` to `state(t+5ns)` is governed by `a(t),b(t)` and `state(t)`. For instance, at 85 ns: `a=1,b=1,state=0`; the next state at 90 ns is 0. At 95 ns: `a=1,b=1,state=1`; the next state at 100 ns is 1. So for `a=1,b=1`, the state holds. At 115 ns: `a=1,b=0,state=1`; next state at 120 ns is 0. At 125 ns: `a=0,b=1,state=1`; next state at 130 ns is 0. At 135 ns: `a=0,b=0,state=1`; next state at 140 ns is 1. At 145 ns: `a=0,b=0,state=0`; next state at 150 ns is 0.

The checker should therefore treat the trace as a sequence of rising-edge samples, using the row’s `a,b` and `state` to predict the next row’s `state`, and using the row’s `a,b,state` to predict `q`. The mid-input changes are irrelevant to the sampled transition except insofar as they determine the next row’s pre-edge values; the public spec says they occur after sampling and are carried to the start of the next row.
