The public table mixes two different observables: `state`, the flip-flop’s stored bit after the sampled rising edge, and `q`, a combinational output. The checker should not assume that `q` is merely the registered state delayed by one cycle. In the rows where both are defined, `q` tracks `a ^ b ^ state` exactly. For example, at 45 ns, `a=0,b=1,state=0` gives `q=1`; at 55 ns, `a=1,b=0,state=0` gives `q=1`; at 65 ns, `a=1,b=1,state=0` gives `q=0`; at 75 ns, `a=0,b=0,state=1` gives `q=1`. This is a strong, directly checkable combinational relation.

The next-state behavior is less explicit. The table shows the state after each rising edge, but it does not label a separate next-state signal. A naive checker that tries to infer `state_next` from `q` on the previous row will encounter apparent contradictions. For instance, at 55 ns the state remains 0 even though the previous sampled `q` at 45 ns was 1. That does not invalidate the table; it simply means the state update is not identical to the previous `q` under every input combination. The checker should therefore treat any next-state equation as a hypothesis to be tested only where the public rows support it, not as a universal contract.

The long all-zero windows are useful. From 10–40 ns and again from 145–190 ns, `a=0,b=0` and both `state` and `q` stay 0. This rules out free-running toggling or an autonomous counter. It supports a hold-like behavior when both inputs are low, but it does not prove a complete next-state function. A safe checker assertion is: when `a=0,b=0`, a defined state does not change across a rising edge, at least for the observed rows.

The initial `x` values at 0–10 ns should be treated as uninitialized power-up, not as a specified reset value. The first defined sampled state appears at 15 ns as 0. The checker should skip rows containing `x` and begin validation once defined values appear, rather than assuming a power-on state.

A short worked example: at 85 ns, `a=1,b=1,state=0`, so `q = 1 ^ 1 ^ 0 = 0`, matching the table. At 95 ns, `a=1,b=1,state=1`, so `q = 1 ^ 1 ^ 1 = 1`, also matching. The state at 95 ns is 1, which is consistent with the table but not derivable from a simple “state equals previous q” rule applied to every edge. This is exactly where an overzealous checker would produce false failures.

For the final Python checker, the useful focus is: skip `x` rows; assert `q == a ^ b ^ state` on every defined row; assert that `a=0,b=0` preserves a defined state across a rising edge; and treat any next-state relation as conditional or soft, not as a universal contract. The initial `x` region is uninitialized power-up, not a reset specification.
