One implementation mistake worth flagging for the final checker is treating the eight flip-flops as a single atomic byte rather than as eight independent one-bit state elements that happen to share a clock and a reset. The public spec says "Create 8 D flip-flops with active high synchronous reset setting the output to zero," and the port widths are `d[8]`, `reset[1]`, `q[8]`. That phrasing is compatible with a byte-wide register, and for a purely functional model the two views coincide on every legal input. The distinction matters only at the boundary the checker is most likely to probe: the relationship between the sampled edge, the reset, and the per-bit data.

The subtle failure mode is a checker that validates `q` as a whole byte against a single expected value derived from `reset` and `d`, but then, when carrying state forward, applies a mid-cycle override to only part of the byte or to the wrong bit positions. Because the spec allows an "r-bit override that could be applied between edges," a mid-cycle change may touch a subset of the eight data bits. If the checker models the register as one byte and the override as one byte, it can silently mask or misplace bits. The correct bookkeeping is per-bit: each of the eight D inputs is captured independently at the rising edge, and each of the eight Q outputs is updated independently, all gated by the same synchronous reset. A mid-cycle change to `d` after sampling must be carried to the next edge bit-for-bit, not collapsed into a byte-level "last value."

A short worked example shows where a byte-atomic checker diverges. Suppose at edge 1 the captured inputs are `d=0xF0`, `reset=0`, so expected `q=0xF0`. After sampling, a mid-cycle override changes only the low nibble of `d` to `0x0F`, giving `d=0xFF` for the next edge, with `reset` still 0. At edge 2 the expected `q` is `0xFF`. A checker that stored the override as a byte and then, say, applied it with a mask error—or that validated edge 1 against the post-override byte—would report a false mismatch at edge 1 or a false pass at edge 2. The per-bit view makes the carry unambiguous: bits 7..4 keep `0xF`, bits 3..0 become `0xF`.

The practical guidance for the final checker is to keep the expected next-state vector as eight independent bits, update it only at the sampled edge using the captured `reset` and `d`, and apply any mid-cycle override to that vector before the next sample. The reset is synchronous and active high, so it must be evaluated at the edge, not as an asynchronous clear; a checker that zeroes `q` on a mid-cycle reset assertion would be wrong. Also, since the spec defines no power-up value, the checker must not assume `q=0x00` before the first sampled edge, and it must not treat a `0x00` result with `reset=0`, `d=0x00` as evidence of a reset. Classify each edge by the captured `reset` and `d`, then compare the settled `q`.
