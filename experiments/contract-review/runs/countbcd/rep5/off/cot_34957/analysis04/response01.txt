The interface has one 1-bit input, `reset`, and two outputs: `q` is 16 bits wide, and `ena` is 3 bits wide. The 16-bit `q` is partitioned into four 4-bit BCD digits. By the stated convention, `q[3:0]` is the ones digit, `q[7:4]` the tens digit, `q[11:8]` the hundreds digit, and `q[15:12]` the thousands digit. Each digit is expected to hold a legal BCD value in the range 0 through 9; values 10 through 15 are outside the intended decimal encoding and should not be produced by a correct counter. The 3-bit `ena` output corresponds to the upper three digits: presumably `ena[0]` enables the tens digit, `ena[1]` the hundreds digit, and `ena[2]` the thousands digit. The public specification does not explicitly map the bit positions, so the checker should treat that mapping as the natural ascending-digit interpretation and remain alert to any ambiguity.

The counter is synchronous with respect to `clk`, and sampling occurs after the positive edge has settled. Each row’s input values are driven before the sampled edge. Optional mid-cycle input changes occur after sampling and before the next edge; they are carried forward to the start of the next row and must not affect the row already sampled. This timing distinction matters because `ena` is a combinational function of the pre-edge state, while `q` is the registered post-edge state.

The boundary behavior is the central issue. For the ones digit, the increment is unconditional modulo 10 when reset is low. For the tens digit, the increment condition is that the pre-edge ones digit equals 9. For the hundreds digit, the condition is that both the pre-edge ones and tens digits equal 9. For the thousands digit, the condition is that the pre-edge ones, tens, and hundreds digits all equal 9. Thus the enables are not derived from the post-edge `q`; they are derived from the captured pre-edge `q`. A checker must therefore capture the pre-edge `q`, compute the expected enables from that captured value, and compare them against the sampled `ena` for the same row. Deriving enables from the post-edge `q` would be wrong: after a carry, the lower digits have wrapped to zero, so the enable conditions would no longer hold even though the enables were legitimately asserted at the edge.

The state update follows standard BCD ripple-carry semantics when `reset` is low at the sampled edge. The ones digit increments modulo 10. The tens digit increments only if the pre-edge ones digit was 9; otherwise it holds. The hundreds digit increments only if the pre-edge ones and tens digits were both 9; otherwise it holds. The thousands digit increments only if the pre-edge ones, tens, and hundreds digits were all 9; otherwise it holds. Each incrementing digit wraps from 9 to 0, and non-carrying digits retain their value. When `reset` is high at the sampled edge, the entire update is overridden and all four digits become zero, regardless of the pre-edge state or the enable values.

A short worked example clarifies the ordering. Suppose the pre-edge state is `q = 0x0099`, so ones = 9 and tens = 9, with `reset = 0`. The expected enables are `ena = 3'b011`: the ones and tens digits are 9, but the hundreds digit is not, so only the tens and hundreds increment enables are asserted. After the sampled edge, the ones and tens wrap to 0, the hundreds increments to 1, and the thousands stays 0, giving `q = 0x0100`. Any mid-input change after sampling is irrelevant to this row and only affects the next sampled edge.

For the final Python checker, the key modeling decision is to separate pre-edge evaluation from post-edge state update. The checker should latch the pre-edge digits for enable comparison, apply synchronous reset with priority over the counting update, and ignore mid-cycle input changes until the next sampled edge. This ordering—capture, evaluate enables, then update state—is what makes the checker consistent with the hardware’s combinational-versus-sequential split.
