One plausible implementation mistake is to derive the three enable outputs from the counter's *next-state* logic rather than from the settled current state. In a ripple-carry BCD counter, the natural way to write the increment equations is to compute carry conditions while deciding which digits to advance: the ones digit rolls over when it is 9, the tens digit advances when the ones digit is 9, the hundreds digit advances when tens and ones are both 9, and so on. If the designer reuses those internal carry terms directly as `ena`, the enables can end up describing the carry that *was consumed* to produce the new `q`, or the carry that will be consumed on the following edge, instead of describing the settled `q` that the checker observes.

This is easy to do accidentally because the same predicate "ones == 9" appears in two roles. In the state-update path it is the condition for propagating a carry into the tens digit on this edge. In the enable path it is the condition that `ena[0]` must report after the edge. Those two roles coincide only when the ones digit is 9 both before and after the edge, which is generally false. After a 9 rolls to 0, the pre-edge predicate is true but the post-edge predicate is false; after an 8 becomes 9, the pre-edge predicate is false but the post-edge predicate is true. A design that wires the pre-edge carry predicate to `ena` will therefore be wrong on exactly the transitions where the digit crosses the 9/0 boundary, while its `q` output remains perfectly correct.

The worked example in the specification illustrates the second direction: prior `q = 0x0098`, settled `q = 0x0099`, correct `ena = 3'b011`. A pre-edge-derived enable would give `ena = 3'b000`, since no lower digit was 9 before the edge. The symmetric error occurs going from `0x0099` to `0x0100`: the pre-edge carry terms are all true, so a pre-edge-derived enable would assert all three bits, but the correct settled enables are all zero because the new digits are 0, 0, and 1. In both cases `q` is right and only `ena` is wrong.

For the final Python checker, this argues for treating `ena` as an independent combinational function of the captured settled `q`, evaluated after reset precedence has been applied. Do not infer enables from the transition, from the prior row, or from any mid-cycle input change. Compute the expected enables directly from the captured `q` digits, compare them to the captured `ena`, and keep that comparison separate from the state-transition check. That way a design with correct counting but one-cycle-off enable logic fails on the enable comparison alone, and a design with correct enables but a broken carry chain fails on the transition comparison alone. Folding the two checks together, or deriving expected enables from the same intermediate carry variables used to predict the next state, risks reproducing the very bug the checker is meant to catch.
