The public interface is a 16-bit BCD counter output `q` plus a 3-bit enable vector `ena`, with a single-bit synchronous active-high `reset` and a clock. The output split is fixed: `q[3:0]` is the ones digit, `q[7:4]` the tens, `q[11:8]` the hundreds, and `q[15:12]` the thousands. Each nibble is a legal BCD digit in 0–9; values 10–15 are outside the intended encoding and should not appear in a correct trace. The enable vector maps positionally to the upper three digits: `ena[0]` corresponds to the tens digit, `ena[1]` to the hundreds, and `ena[2]` to the thousands. There is no enable for the ones digit.

Boundary behavior is the main thing to pin down. On a normal row, the ones digit always advances modulo 10. A higher digit advances only when every lower digit is 9 at the sampled edge. Thus `ena[0]` is high exactly when `q[3:0] == 9`; `ena[1]` is high exactly when `q[3:0] == 9` and `q[7:4] == 9`; `ena[2]` is high exactly when the ones, tens, and hundreds digits are all 9. These enables describe the carry into the next state, but they are computed from the current sampled `q`, not from the next value. That is the central checker point: enables are a function of the present state. A design that derives them from the next state, or that asserts a higher enable merely because a lower digit is 9 without checking all lower digits, will fail at boundaries such as `0x0099` (tens and ones 9, hundreds not 9) and `0x0999` (thousands not 9).

The full count sequence runs from `0x0000` through `0x9999`. After `0x9999`, the next normal row is `0x0000`, with all enables low on the `0x9999` row except that `ena[2]` is high because the thousands digit will wrap. More precisely, at `0x9999` all three enables are high, and the next state is `0x0000`. At `0x1000`, the thousands digit has just wrapped from 9 to 0 on the preceding count, and the next normal row is `0x1001`.

Reset is synchronous and active-high. When the sampled `reset` is high at a clock edge, the row is a reset row: both `q` and `ena` are zero, independent of prior state. Mid-row changes to `reset` occur after sampling and apply to the following row, so the checker must latch the sampled reset per row. A high-to-low change after sampling leaves the current row as a reset row and makes the next row the first normal row; a low-to-high change makes the current row normal and the next row a reset row. The checker should compare only sampled rows, compute expected enables from the current sampled `q` on normal rows, force both outputs to zero on reset rows, and carry mid-row input changes forward without modifying the current row. No asynchronous power-up value should be assumed.

Worked example: suppose the sampled row has `q = 0x0099` and `reset = 0`. The ones and tens digits are 9, the hundreds and thousands are 0. Expected enables are `ena[0] = 1` (ones is 9), `ena[1] = 1` (ones and tens are 9), and `ena[2] = 0` (hundreds is not 9). The next normal row is `0x0100`.
