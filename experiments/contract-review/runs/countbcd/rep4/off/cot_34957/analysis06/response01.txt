One common implementation mistake is to compute the enable signals from the *next* state rather than from the *current* latched state, or to let the reset affect only `q` and not `ena`. The public spec ties `ena` to the digits of the latched `q`: the tens digit should be incremented exactly when the ones digit is 9, the hundreds digit when ones and tens are both 9, and the thousands digit when the lower three digits are all 9. If a design instead derives `ena` from the incremented value, the timing of the enables shifts by one cycle relative to the digits they describe, and the checker will see enables asserted on the wrong row. Similarly, if reset clears `q` but leaves `ena` reflecting the pre-reset digits, the row immediately after a reset posedge will show nonzero enables alongside `q=0`, which violates the requirement that reset gate all three enable bits to zero.

A related trap is treating `ena` as a registered output that lags `q` by a cycle. The spec describes `ena` as indicating when each upper digit *should be incremented*, i.e., it is a combinational function of the latched digits presented in the same sampled row as `q`. A checker that expects `ena` to correspond to the previous row's digits, or that allows a one-cycle skew between `q` and `ena`, will accept a design that is off by one and reject a correct one.

The final checker should therefore, at each posedge, latch `reset` and `q`, then compute expected `ena` directly from the latched `q` digits (ones==9 for `ena[0]`, ones==9 and tens==9 for `ena[1]`, ones==9 and tens==9 and hundreds==9 for `ena[2]`), force all three to zero when the latched `reset` is 1, and compare against the sampled `ena` in that same row. Expected next `q` is the BCD increment of the latched `q`, or zero under reset. No power-up assumption should be made, and mid-cycle `reset` changes must be deferred to the next posedge.
