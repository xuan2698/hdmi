The public interface is a synchronous BCD counter with a single 1-bit input `reset` and two outputs: a 16-bit `q` and a 3-bit `ena`. The 16-bit `q` is partitioned into four 4-bit BCD digits: `q[3:0]` is the ones digit, `q[7:4]` the tens, `q[11:8]` the hundreds, and `q[15:12]` the thousands. Each digit is constrained to the legal BCD range 0–9; values 10–15 are not valid counter states. The 3-bit `ena` corresponds to the upper three digits: `ena[0]` signals that the tens digit should increment, `ena[1]` the hundreds, and `ena[2]` the thousands. There is no enable bit for the ones digit, since the ones digit increments every non-reset cycle.

Boundary behavior is where the checker must be most careful. The ones digit rolls over from 9 to 0 and carries into the tens digit; the tens digit rolls over from 9 to 0 and carries into the hundreds; and so on. The enable signals are carry conditions derived from the current latched count, not from the next count. Specifically, `ena[0]` is asserted when the ones digit equals 9, `ena[1]` when both the ones and tens digits equal 9, and `ena[2]` when the ones, tens, and hundreds digits all equal 9. These conditions describe whether the corresponding upper digit will change on the next increment. The thousands digit has no enable output because there is no fifth digit to carry into; it simply wraps from 9 to 0.

The reset input is synchronous and active-high. When a posedge samples `reset=1`, the next state of `q` is zero and all three `ena` bits are zero. This reset gating is essential: a checker that computes `ena` purely from `q` without considering reset will incorrectly expect a carry enable on a reset cycle where the pre-edge count happens to end in one or more 9s. The correct expectation is: if the latched `reset` is 1, next `q=0` and all `ena=0`; otherwise next `q` is the BCD increment of the latched `q`, and `ena` reflects the carry conditions of the latched `q`.

Power-up is unspecified. The specification gives no initial value for `q` or `ena` and imposes no behavior before the first reset. A checker must not assume `q=0` at time zero or compare `ena` against a guessed initial state. The safe policy is to treat sampled rows as unknown until a `reset=1` posedge has been observed, then begin checking from the known zero state established by that reset. Seeding expectations from an assumed zero without an observed reset can produce false failures against a legal design whose flops power up arbitrarily.

Mid-cycle input changes are not applied immediately. A `reset` that goes high between edges is carried forward and takes effect only at the next posedge. The checker should latch `reset` and `q` at each posedge, compute the expected next `q` and expected `ena` from those latched values, and only then compare against the sampled outputs. Mid-cycle transitions must not influence the expected values for the current row; they belong to the next row's latched inputs.

Worked example: at a posedge the checker latches `q=16'h0029`, `reset=0`. Ones=9, so the tens digit increments: expected next `q=16'h0030`, and `ena[0]=1` this cycle; `ena[1]` and `ena[2]` are 0 because not all lower digits are 9. If `reset` then rises mid-cycle, that change is ignored until the next posedge, where the expected next state becomes `q=0` with all `ena=0`.

The key point for the final checker: latch `reset` and `q` at each posedge, derive expected next `q` and expected `ena` solely from those latched values (with reset gating `ena` to zero), require an observed reset before trusting any state, and never let mid-cycle transitions or an assumed power-up value affect expectations.
