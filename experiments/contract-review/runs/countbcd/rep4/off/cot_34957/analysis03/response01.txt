The counter is a synchronous BCD ripple-carry chain. At each posedge, the ones digit increments modulo 10; when the ones digit is 9, it wraps to 0 and the tens digit increments; when both ones and tens are 9, the hundreds digit increments; and so on. The upper three digits therefore increment only on the appropriate all-lower-digits-9 condition. The `ena` outputs are combinational functions of the pre-edge `q` (and reset), not of the post-edge state: `ena[0]` is high exactly when the tens digit will increment on this edge, i.e. when the ones digit is 9 and reset is low; `ena[1]` is high when ones and tens are both 9 and reset is low; `ena[2]` is high when ones, tens, and hundreds are all 9 and reset is low. Because `ena` describes the increment the current edge causes, it must be derived from the latched pre-edge `q`, never from the new `q`.

Reset is synchronous and active-high. On a posedge with `reset=1`, the next state is `q=0` regardless of the current count, and the natural expectation is `ena=0` for all three bits, since the clear overrides any carry that would otherwise have occurred. A checker that computes `ena` from `q` alone without gating on reset will wrongly expect a carry enable on a reset cycle where the pre-edge count happens to end in 9s. The correct expectation is: if latched `reset` is 1, next `q=0` and all `ena=0`; otherwise next `q` is the BCD increment of latched `q`, and `ena` reflects the carry conditions of latched `q`.

Power-up is unspecified. The spec gives no initial value for `q` or `ena` and imposes no behavior before the first reset. A checker must not assume `q=0` at time zero or compare `ena` against a guessed initial state. The safe policy is to treat sampled rows as unknown until a `reset=1` posedge has been observed, then begin checking from the known zero state established by that reset. Seeding expectations from an assumed zero without an observed reset can produce false failures against a legal design whose flops power up arbitrarily.

Mid-cycle input changes are not applied immediately. A `reset` that goes high between edges is carried forward and takes effect only at the next posedge. The checker should latch `reset` and `q` at each posedge, compute the expected next `q` and expected `ena` from those latched values, and only then compare against the sampled outputs. Mid-cycle transitions must not influence the expected values for the current row; they belong to the next row's latched inputs.

Worked example: at a posedge the checker latches `q=16'h0029`, `reset=0`. Ones=9, so the tens digit increments: expected next `q=16'h0030`, and `ena[0]=1` this cycle; `ena[1]` and `ena[2]` are 0 because not all lower digits are 9. If `reset` then rises mid-cycle, that change is ignored until the next posedge, where the expected next state becomes `q=0` with all `ena=0`.

The key point for the final checker: latch `reset` and `q` at each posedge, derive expected next `q` and expected `ena` solely from those latched values (with reset gating `ena` to zero), require an observed reset before trusting any state, and never let mid-cycle transitions or an assumed power-up value affect expectations.
