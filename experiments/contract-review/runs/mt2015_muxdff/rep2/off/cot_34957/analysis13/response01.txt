The public spec fixes the observable behavior at the three-bit register `q` in `full_module`: on each `posedge clk`, if `L` is high the register loads `r`; otherwise it applies the recurrence `q <= {q[1]^q[2], q[0], q[2]}`. The hierarchical requirement — a flipflop plus 2-1 mux in a submodule instantiated three times — is a structural constraint, but the checker should validate the *functional* contract at the top-level ports, not the internal hierarchy. The submodule's per-bit ports are `L`, `q_in`, `r_in` (each 1 bit) and `Q` (1 bit), clocked by `clk`. That means each instantiation handles one bit position, and the parent must wire the XOR term for bit 2 externally (or via a small combinational helper) since the submodule only sees a single `q_in`.

Timing is the critical axis. The checker drives `L` and `r` before the rising edge, then samples `q` after the edge has settled. Any extra register stage in the parent — for example, latching `L` or `r_in` through an additional flop before presenting it to the submodule's `D` — shifts the effective load by one cycle. The first `L=1` row would then appear to have no effect, and the checker would observe the unknown seed persisting one row longer than expected. Similarly, if the parent computes the XOR from *registered* copies of `q[1]` and `q[2]` rather than the current outputs, the recurrence is delayed. The checker should therefore treat the top-level `q` as the single source of truth and compare against a direct software model of the recurrence, seeded unknown until the first full `L=1` load.

Mid-input changes occur after sampling and before the next edge; they are carried into the next row's drive. This matters because a mid-cycle change to `L` or `r` must not affect the already-sampled `q`. A design that samples `L` combinationally into the submodule's enable (rather than at the edge) could glitch, but since the submodule is specified as edge-triggered, the checker's model should only evaluate inputs at the edge.

A useful worked example for the checker: load `r=101` with `L=1`, giving `q=101`. Then drive `L=0`. Correct next state is `{q[1]^q[2], q[0], q[2]} = {0^1, 1, 1} = 110`. A buggy design that treats the recurrence as an identity permutation yields `101`, which differs. But note that loads of `000` or `111` make correct and buggy designs agree, so the checker must include at least one `L=0` step after a load where `q[1] != q[2]` to exercise the XOR polarity. Equally, the checker should not assume the submodule's internal port order matches the concatenation order; it should derive expected `q` solely from the public recurrence and the sampled edge timing.
