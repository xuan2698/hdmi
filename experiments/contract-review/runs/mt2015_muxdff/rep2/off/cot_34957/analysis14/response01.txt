The public spec fixes the observable behavior at the top level: on each rising edge, if `L` is high the three-bit state `q` becomes `r`; otherwise `q` becomes `{q[1]^q[2], q[0], q[2]}`. The hierarchical requirement — one flipflop plus a 2:1 mux per bit, instantiated three times — is an implementation constraint, not an observable one. For the final checker, the safest stance is to model only the top-level recurrence and the edge timing, and to treat the internal submodule boundary as invisible. That keeps the checker from over-constraining port order, internal signal names, or whether the XOR term for bit 2 is computed in the parent or in a helper.

Reset and power-up are the subtle part. The public spec shows no reset port and no initial value for `q`. In Verilog simulation, an uninitialized `reg` starts as `x`, so before the first `L=1` load the top-level `q` is unknown. The checker should therefore seed its reference model as unknown and only begin strict comparison once a load has occurred. A design that powers up to `000` is not wrong under this spec, but neither is one that powers up to `xxx`; the checker must not demand a particular power-up value. The first `L=1` row is the synchronization point: after that edge, `q` must equal `r` exactly, and from then on the recurrence applies.

Timing is the critical axis. The checker drives `L` and `r` before the rising edge, then samples `q` after the edge has settled. Any extra register stage in the parent — for example, latching `L` or `r_in` through an additional flop before presenting it to the submodule's `D` — shifts the effective load by one cycle. The first `L=1` row would then appear to have no effect, and the checker would observe the unknown seed persisting one row longer than expected. Similarly, if the parent computes the XOR from registered copies of `q[1]` and `q[2]` rather than the current outputs, the recurrence is delayed. The checker should treat the top-level `q` as the single source of truth and compare against a direct software model of the recurrence, seeded unknown until the first full `L=1` load.

Mid-input changes occur after sampling and before the next edge; they are carried into the next row's drive. This matters because a mid-cycle change to `L` or `r` must not affect the already-sampled `q`. A design that samples `L` combinationally into the submodule's enable rather than at the edge could glitch, but since the submodule is specified as edge-triggered, the checker's model should only evaluate inputs at the edge.

A useful worked example: load `r=101` with `L=1`, giving `q=101`. Then drive `L=0`. Correct next state is `{q[1]^q[2], q[0], q[2]} = {0^1, 1, 1} = 110`. A buggy design that treats the recurrence as an identity permutation yields `101`, which differs. But loads of `000` or `111` make correct and buggy designs agree, so the checker must include at least one `L=0` step after a load where `q[1] != q[2]` to exercise the XOR polarity. Equally, the checker should not assume the submodule's internal port order matches the concatenation order; it should derive expected `q` solely from the public recurrence and the sampled edge timing.
