The public spec's next-state function under `L = 0` is `{q[1]^q[2], q[0], q[2]}`. The two absorbing states `000` and `111` are useless for discrimination: `000` maps to `000`, and `111` maps to `{1^1,1,1} = 111`. A swapped XOR input, a dropped `q[0]`, or a misrouted `q[2]` all leave these fixed points intact. Mixed states are required. The spec's own example is the canonical probe: from `q = 110` with `L = 0`, the correct next state is `{1^1, 0, 1} = 001`; a single miswire on the low bit yields `011`, a one-bit error that only a full-vector comparison catches. The checker should therefore drive a sequence of mixed states under `L = 0`, including states where `q[1]^q[2]` differs from both `q[0]` and `q[2]`, so the XOR result, the shifted `q[0]`, and the copied `q[2]` each land in a distinguishable position.

Timing is the second trap, and it interacts with state choice. Inputs are driven before the sampled posedge; the sample is taken after the edge settles. The checker must latch the three old `q` bits at that sample, compute the expected next state from the latched bits plus the current `L`/`r`, and compare against the settled `Q` for the same row. It must not re-read `q` after the edge, because `q` has already advanced, and it must not fold a mid-cycle change into the current row—that change belongs to the next row. Since no reset is assumed, the harness-declared initial `q` is the starting state, so the first row's expected value depends on that initial vector; choosing a mixed initial state (e.g. `110`) makes the very first comparison discriminating.

A useful final-checker heuristic: prefer vectors where the three bits of `q` are not all equal, and where `q[1]^q[2]` differs from `q[0]` and from `q[2]`. Under those conditions, any single-bit miswire in the three instances produces a settled `Q` that differs from the expected full vector, and a full-vector equality check flags it. Per-bit checks or structural inspection would miss exactly these width-legal errors.
