One common implementation mistake is to let the enable path run before reset is applied, or to apply reset only to the seconds/minutes counters while allowing the hour counter and `pm` flag to advance. In a 12-hour clock this is especially damaging because the hour rollover is not a simple modulo operation: the sequence is `12→01→…→11→12`, and `pm` must toggle exactly when the hour moves from `11` to `12` or from `12` to `01`. If reset is treated as a lower-priority condition, a cycle with `reset=1` and `ena=1` can produce a mixed state: the seconds and minutes may be cleared, but the hour may still carry and `pm` may still flip. The result is not merely an off-by-one count; it is an invalid clock state that can persist and corrupt subsequent transitions.

A short worked example makes the failure visible. Suppose the clock is at `11:59:59 AM` and the next sampled edge has both `reset=1` and `ena=1`. Correct behavior is `12:00:00 AM` with `pm=0`, because reset has higher priority than enable and must suppress all counting. A faulty enable-first implementation may first increment seconds `59→00`, minutes `59→00`, hours `11→12`, and then toggle `pm` from AM to PM, producing `12:00:00 PM`. That is wrong: reset must suppress the entire increment, including the hour carry and the `pm` flip. The final checker should therefore evaluate reset as an unconditional first branch at every sampled posedge, before any enable or carry logic, and compare outputs against the reset constant `12:00:00 AM` with `pm=0`.

This example also exposes why the checker needs both structural and semantic invariants. Structurally, each of `hh`, `mm`, and `ss` must be valid BCD in range: hours `01`–`12`, minutes and seconds `00`–`59`. Semantically, `pm` must flip on exactly the two hour transitions `11→12` and `12→01`, and must hold on every other enabled increment and on every disabled increment. A checker that only validates numeric ranges can miss a stuck or inverted `pm`; a checker that only validates `pm` can miss a malformed BCD hour such as `0x13` or `0x00`. Both classes of check are needed, and the reset branch must dominate them.

The timing protocol adds a second trap. Mid-cycle input changes occur after sampling and before the other edge, and are carried to the start of the next row. The checker should snapshot driven inputs at each posedge, evaluate the transition from that snapshot, compare outputs, and only then fold mid-cycle changes into the pending-input register for the next row. A post-edge reset must not retroactively alter the row already sampled. Likewise, power-up state is unspecified until the first sampled `reset=1` edge establishes `12:00:00 AM`; the checker should not compare outputs on earlier rows.

The highest-value focus for the final checker is thus an unconditional, first-priority reset branch at every posedge, paired with BCD/range invariants and explicit `pm` flip checks on `11→12` and `12→01`. The `reset=1, ena=1` collision at `11:59:59 AM` is the sharpest single test: it distinguishes correct reset-priority logic from an enable-first model in one row, and it simultaneously guards the `pm` transition that a range-only checker would overlook.
