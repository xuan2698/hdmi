The public interface fixes the port widths tightly enough that the checker can treat every counter as a small finite-state machine. `reset` and `ena` are each one bit, sampled at the rising edge of `clk`. The outputs are `pm` (one bit), and `hh`, `mm`, `ss` (eight bits each). Each eight-bit output encodes two BCD digits: the high nibble is the tens digit and the low nibble is the units digit. For `hh`, the legal displayed range is 01 through 12, so the high nibble is 0 or 1 and the low nibble is constrained by the 12-hour wrap. For `mm` and `ss`, the legal range is 00 through 59, so the high nibble is 0 through 5 and the low nibble is 0 through 9. The `pm` bit is independent of the BCD encoding and must be consistent with the 12-hour convention: 12:xx AM and 12:xx PM are the boundary hours, and the indicator flips when the hour advances from 11 to 12.

Boundary values deserve explicit attention because they are where BCD and range checks interact with the 12-hour wrap. The legal hour sequence is 12 AM, 01 AM, ..., 11 AM, 12 PM, 01 PM, ..., 11 PM, then back to 12 AM. Thus `hh=0x12` with `pm=0` is the reset state, `hh=0x11` with `pm=1` is the last state before the AM rollover, and `hh=0x12` with `pm=1` is noon. A checker that only asserts `hh` is in 01..12 and BCD-valid will accept `hh=0x00`, which is not a legal displayed hour, so the hour range check must exclude zero. Similarly, `mm=0x60` or `ss=0x6A` are BCD-invalid and out of range; both conditions should be checked, but the range check alone catches the tens digit while the BCD check catches the units digit.

The key ordering point is reset versus enable at each sampled rising edge. Because reset is synchronous, active high, and has higher priority than enable—and because it must take effect even when `ena` is low—the checker must decide the expected next state from the sampled `reset` input before considering any increment logic. If the sampled `reset` is high, the expected next state is exactly `12:00:00 AM`: `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, and `pm=0`. This expectation is unconditional with respect to the prior counter values and with respect to `ena`. Only when the sampled `reset` is low should the checker evaluate the enabled transition, then apply BCD/range and `pm` flip/hold checks.

This ordering matters because range and BCD checks alone are insufficient to detect reset-priority bugs. A design that gates reset behind `ena` can remain at a perfectly legal-looking state such as `11:59:59 PM` across a reset edge. Every digit is in range and BCD-valid, so a checker that only validates output encoding would pass it. The reset-priority expectation is what catches the error. Similarly, a design that increments before applying reset on an enabled reset edge can produce a transient legal-looking state such as `12:00:00 PM` or `12:00:01 AM`. Again, BCD and range checks may not flag it; only the exact reset expectation does. The checker should therefore snapshot the driven inputs at the posedge, branch on reset first, and compare outputs to the reset expectation without consulting prior counters or `ena`. If reset is low, it computes the enabled transition and then applies independent BCD/range and `pm` assertions.

Mid-input changes occur after sampling and before the other edge, and are carried to the start of the next row. They must be merged only after the current row is evaluated, so a reset asserted after sampling cannot retroactively rewrite the already-checked row. Rows before the first sampled `reset=1` edge should be skipped because power-up state is unspecified. The useful checker order is: reset branch first, then transition, then BCD/range and `pm` checks.
