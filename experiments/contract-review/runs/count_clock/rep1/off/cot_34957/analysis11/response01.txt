A useful distinguishing example for the final checker is a single enabled edge that crosses a minute boundary while the hour is `0x11`, followed by another enabled edge that crosses the next minute boundary while the hour is `0x12`. This pair isolates the hour-carry asymmetry and the `pm` toggle rule.

Suppose the sampled state before an enabled edge is `hh=0x11`, `mm=0x59`, `ss=0x59`, `pm=0`, with `reset=0` and `ena=1`. The seconds digit must wrap from `0x59` to `0x00`, the minutes digit must wrap from `0x59` to `0x00`, and the hour must advance from `0x11` to `0x12`. Because the hour did not wrap from `0x12` to `0x01`, `pm` must remain `0`. The next sampled state is therefore `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, `pm=0`.

Now hold `reset=0` and `ena=1` for the next sampled edge. The state before that edge is `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, `pm=0`. The seconds increment to `0x01`, the minutes remain `0x00`, and the hour remains `0x12`; no carry reaches the hour. This edge is not the interesting one. The interesting second edge is instead an enabled edge from `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x59`, `ss=0x59`, `pm=0`. On that edge, seconds wrap to `0x00`, minutes wrap to `0x00`, and the hour wraps from `0x12` to `0x01`. Because the hour wrapped through 12, `pm` must toggle from `0` to `1`. The next sampled state is `hh=0x01`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, `pm=1`.

This example is useful because it forces the checker to distinguish two superficially similar events: a minute carry at hour `0x11` and a minute carry at hour `0x12`. In both cases `mm` and `ss` wrap from `0x59` to `0x00`. In both cases the hour changes. But only the second case toggles `pm`. A checker that keys `pm` toggling solely on “a minute carry occurred” will incorrectly toggle `pm` on the `0x11→0x12` transition. A checker that keys `pm` toggling solely on “the hour changed” will make the same mistake. The correct condition is specifically that the hour wrapped from `0x12` to `0x01`.

The example also exercises the BCD boundary codes that matter most. The seconds and minutes transitions `0x59→0x00` are not binary increments; they are decimal wraps that emit carries. The hour transitions `0x11→0x12` and `0x12→0x01` are legal BCD hour steps, while `0x00` and `0x13` are illegal hour codes. The checker should reject any sampled next state containing `hh=0x00` or `hh=0x13`, even transiently, because the public specification defines hours as `01` through `12`.

Priority must also be respected in this example. If `reset=1` is sampled on either edge, the next state must be `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, `pm=0`, regardless of `ena`. If `reset=0` and `ena=0`, all outputs must hold their previous sampled values. Only `reset=0, ena=1` advances the counter. Because `ena` is not assumed periodic, the checker must compare each enabled edge against the immediately preceding sampled state, not against an assumed one-second cadence.

Finally, the checker should not assume the first sampled row is 12:00 AM unless a reset was actually sampled at that edge. The initial state is whatever the first sampled outputs are, provided they are legal. Reset is the only mechanism that guarantees 12:00 AM.
