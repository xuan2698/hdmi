The key distinguishing case for the final checker is a held enable across multiple sampled edges, because it separates a correct level-sensitive interpretation of `ena` from an incorrect edge-detection interpretation. Consider a testbench that raises `ena` and leaves it high for several rising edges of `clk`. The public specification says the counters increment whenever `ena` is high at a sampled edge, so each such edge should advance the clock by one second. A checker that treats `ena` as a one-shot pulse—advancing only on the first high sample and then waiting for a low-to-high transition—will diverge immediately: after two or three held-high edges, its internal time will lag the DUT by one or two seconds, and every subsequent comparison will fail even though the DUT is behaving correctly.

This matters especially because the specification explicitly warns that a “pulse” may remain high across more than one sampled rising edge. The intended semantics are therefore level-based at the sampling instant, not edge-based. The final Python checker should capture the pre-edge value of `ena` (and `reset`) together with the pre-edge registered outputs `hh`, `mm`, `ss`, and `pm`, compute the expected next state from that snapshot, and only then compare against the post-edge settled outputs. It must not infer an increment from a transition of `ena`; it must simply ask whether `ena` was high at the edge.

A second, related timing hazard is mid-row input changes. The protocol states that optional mid input changes occur after sampling and before the other edge, and are carried to the start of the next row. If the checker reads inputs after the edge has settled, it may accidentally use a mid-row change as though it belonged to the current transition. That produces false mismatches near boundaries—for example, when `ena` is asserted mid-row just after an edge where it was low. The correct discipline is to latch the pre-edge controls and outputs, compute the expected transition from that latched snapshot, and compare only the registered outputs after settling. Mid-row changes then naturally apply to the following row.

A short worked example illustrates both points. Suppose the pre-edge state is `hh=12`, `mm=59`, `ss=59`, `pm=1`, with `reset=0` and `ena=1`. The expected next state is `hh=01`, `mm=00`, `ss=00`, `pm=1`: seconds and minutes wrap with carry, hour 12 becomes 01, and PM is unchanged because the AM/PM toggle occurs only at the 11:59:59→12:00:00 boundary. If `ena` were held high for the next edge as well, the checker must again increment from `01:00:00` to `01:00:01`, not wait for a new rising edge of `ena`. If instead `reset=1` at the sampled edge, the expected next state is `12:00:00` with `pm=0`, regardless of `ena`, since reset has higher priority.

For the final checker, the useful focus is therefore: sample controls and outputs before the edge, treat `ena` as a level, apply reset with priority, compute BCD carries and the 12-hour/AM-PM rule from the snapshot, and compare after settling. The main false-failure risks are post-edge input contamination and one-shot enable modeling; making pre-edge versus mid-row timing explicit avoids both.
