The public interface is small but the timing semantics matter more than the widths. Inputs are `reset` and `ena`, each one bit. Outputs are `pm` (one bit), `hh` (8 bits), `mm` (8 bits), and `ss` (8 bits). The 8-bit widths are exactly enough for two BCD digits: four bits per digit, so values are not binary integers but packed decimal. For example, `hh = 8'h12` means hour 12, not decimal 18. The checker must decode and validate nibbles separately rather than treating `hh`, `mm`, or `ss` as ordinary 0–255 counters.

The legal output domains are narrow. `hh` must represent 01 through 12 in BCD, so the high nibble is 0 or 1 and the low nibble is constrained by the 12-hour range. `mm` and `ss` must represent 00 through 59 in BCD, so each high nibble is 0–5 and each low nibble is 0–9. `pm` is a single bit: 0 for AM, 1 for PM. Reset is synchronous and active high, with priority over `ena`; when reset is sampled high at a rising edge, the next registered outputs are 12:00:00 AM, i.e. `hh=8'h12`, `mm=8'h00`, `ss=8'h00`, `pm=0`. Reset can occur while `ena` is low, so the checker must not require enable for reset to take effect.

The critical boundary point is that `ena` is a level sampled at the clock edge, not an edge-detected one-shot. The specification says the counters are clocked by a fast `clk`, with a pulse on `ena` whenever the clock should increment. In simulation, a “pulse” may remain high across more than one sampled rising edge. The intended behavior is that each sampled edge with `ena` high advances the clock by one second. A checker that advances only on the first high sample of a high interval will produce false mismatches whenever the testbench holds `ena` high for multiple edges. The final Python checker should therefore evaluate `ena` as a pre-edge level: if `reset` is low and `ena` is high at the sampled edge, increment; if `ena` is low, hold.

The increment rules are BCD with carry. Seconds go 00–59, then wrap to 00 and carry into minutes. Minutes go 00–59, then wrap to 00 and carry into hours. Hours go 01–12 with a 12-hour wrap: after 11:59:59 the next state is 12:00:00 with `pm` toggled; after 12:59:59 the next state is 01:00:00 with `pm` unchanged. Thus `pm` changes only at the 11:59:59→12:00:00 boundary, not at 12:59:59→01:00:00. Reset forces AM independently of the prior `pm`.

A short worked example: pre-edge state `hh=8'h12`, `mm=8'h59`, `ss=8'h59`, `pm=1`, with `reset=0`, `ena=1`. The expected next state is `hh=8'h01`, `mm=8'h00`, `ss=8'h00`, `pm=1`. Seconds and minutes wrap with carry, hour 12 becomes 01, and PM remains asserted. If `reset=1` at that same edge, the expected next state is `hh=8'h12`, `mm=8'h00`, `ss=8'h00`, `pm=0` regardless of `ena`.

For the final checker, the useful discipline is to capture pre-edge registered outputs and pre-edge control inputs, compute the expected next state from those values, then compare after the edge has settled. Mid-row input changes occur after sampling and belong to the next row; they must not be mixed into the current transition. The checker should also validate BCD digit ranges and the 12-hour domain after every update, and should treat `ena` as a sampled level rather than an edge event. The main false-failure risk is post-edge input contamination or one-shot enable modeling, so pre-edge versus mid-row timing should be explicit.
