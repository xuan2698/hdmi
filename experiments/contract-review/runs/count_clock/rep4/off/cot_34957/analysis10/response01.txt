The public interface is small but the timing semantics are where a checker is most likely to produce false failures. Inputs are `clk`, `reset` (1 bit), and `ena` (1 bit). Outputs are `pm` (1 bit), `hh` (8 bits), `mm` (8 bits), and `ss` (8 bits). The 8-bit widths are exactly two BCD digits each: `hh` encodes 01–12, `mm` and `ss` encode 00–59. Because these are BCD, not binary integers, the checker must not compare or increment them as ordinary 8-bit numbers. For example, `hh=0x12` means twelve, and the successor of `0x09` is `0x10`, not `0x0A`. Boundary values that matter are `ss=0x59` rolling to `0x00` with a minute carry, `mm=0x59` rolling to `0x00` with an hour carry, `hh=0x12` rolling to `0x01`, and `hh=0x11` rolling to `0x12` while toggling `pm`. Reset forces `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, `pm=0`, i.e. 12:00 AM, and has priority over `ena`.

The most useful point for the final Python checker is the distinction between pre-edge sampled controls and mid-row input changes. The specification says each row's values are driven before the sampled edge, optional mid input changes occur after sampling and before the other edge, and those changes are carried to the start of the next row. Therefore the checker should latch `reset`, `ena`, and the current outputs from the pre-edge snapshot, compute the expected next state from that snapshot, then compare the registered outputs only after the posedge has settled. If the checker instead reads inputs after settling, a mid-row change on `ena` or `reset` can be misattributed to the transition just sampled, producing false mismatches near boundaries.

A second related point is that `ena` is a level-style increment pulse, not an edge event. If `ena` is high in two consecutive pre-edge snapshots, the clock increments at both sampled edges. The checker must not model `ena` as a one-shot or require a rising transition. Conversely, `ena` low means no increment unless `reset` is high.

Short worked example: pre-edge snapshot `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x59`, `ss=0x59`, `pm=1`, `reset=0`, `ena=1`. Expected next state is `hh=0x01`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`, `pm=1`: seconds and minutes wrap with carry, hour 12 becomes 01, and PM stays because the AM/PM toggle happens at the 11:59:59 to 12:00:00 boundary, not at 12:59:59. If `ena` remains high for the next sampled edge, the next expected state is `01:00:01`, again from the new pre-edge snapshot. If `reset=1` in a snapshot, expected next state is `12:00:00`, `pm=0`, regardless of `ena`.

For the final checker, focus on pre-edge latching, level-sensitive `ena`, reset priority, BCD digit carries, and the 12-hour/AM-PM boundary rule. The main false-failure risks are post-edge input contamination and one-shot enable modeling.
