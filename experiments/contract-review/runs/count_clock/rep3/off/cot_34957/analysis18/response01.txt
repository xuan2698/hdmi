One possible implementation mistake is to treat the 12-hour rollover as a simple modulo-12 increment of the hour counter while handling `pm` independently, without recognizing that the hour sequence is not a uniform modulo-12 cycle in BCD. The legal hour sequence is 01, 02, …, 11, 12, 01, 02, …, and the transition 12→01 is the only place where the tens digit of the hour drops from 1 to 0 while the units digit goes from 2 to 1. A counter that increments the BCD hour as if it were a binary value, or that uses a single “hour == 12” test to decide both wrap and `pm` toggling, can easily produce an off-by-one boundary. The subtle part is that `pm` toggles on the 11→12 transition, not on the 12→01 transition. Thus the hour rollover and the AM/PM rollover occur at different boundaries. An implementation that toggles `pm` whenever the hour wraps from 12 to 01 will be one half-day out of phase; an implementation that toggles `pm` on every hour increment will be badly wrong. A checker must therefore verify the exact BCD hour sequence and the exact `pm` transition point, not merely that hours stay in 01–12 and that `pm` changes sometimes.

A related mistake is to let `ena` dominate `reset` when both are asserted at a sampled edge. The specification states that reset is synchronous, active high, and has higher priority than enable. Therefore, at a posedge where both `reset` and `ena` are high, the next state must be 12:00:00 AM with `pm = 0`, not an incremented time. A naive checker that models the counter as “if ena then increment; if reset then clear” in the wrong order, or that treats reset as merely clearing seconds while leaving hours/minutes/pm alone, will accept an incorrect design. Reset must clear all fields to the 12:00 AM state, including the AM/PM indicator.

A short worked example illustrates the priority issue. Suppose the sampled state is 11:59:59 AM, with `ena = 1` and `reset = 0`. At the next sampled posedge, the expected state is 12:00:00 PM: seconds and minutes roll to 00, hours go from 11 to 12, and `pm` toggles from 0 to 1. A naive checker that increments hours modulo 12 without toggling `pm` on the 11→12 boundary, or that toggles `pm` on every hour rollover, will fail here. The correct rule is that `pm` changes only when hours roll from 11 to 12, not when hours roll from 12 to 01.

Now suppose that after this sampled edge, but before the next edge, `reset` is asserted mid-row while `ena` remains high. The mid-row rule says this change does not retroactively alter the already-sampled 12:00:00 PM outputs. Instead, the asserted reset is carried to the start of the next row and applied at the next sampled posedge. At that next boundary, reset has higher priority than enable, so the expected state is 12:00:00 AM with `pm = 0`, not an incremented state such as 12:00:01 PM. A checker that applies `ena` first and `reset` second, or that lets `ena` dominate when both are high, will disagree. A checker that compares outputs immediately after the mid-row reset assertion will also disagree, because the outputs for the current row were already sampled and should remain 12:00:00 PM until the next boundary.

This example is useful because it separates three concerns that a naive checker often conflates. First, reset is synchronous and active high, so it affects state only at a sampled posedge. Second, reset has higher priority than enable, so simultaneous `reset = 1` and `ena = 1` must yield the reset state rather than an increment. Third, mid-row input changes are pending values for the next sampled edge, not immediate output changes. The final Python checker should therefore maintain pending `reset` and `ena` values, apply them only at the next sampled posedge, evaluate reset before enable, and compare outputs only at posedge boundaries after the sampled edge has settled. It should also avoid inferring an undefined power-up state and should not silently normalize malformed BCD or out-of-range values; the public specification defines the legal ranges and rollover behavior, and the checker should treat deviations as mismatches rather than correcting them.
