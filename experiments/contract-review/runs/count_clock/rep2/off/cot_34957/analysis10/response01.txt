The interface is small but the widths matter. `reset` and `ena` are each one bit, so they are sampled as 0/1 control signals rather than vectors. `pm` is one bit: 0 for AM, 1 for PM. `hh`, `mm`, and `ss` are each eight bits because each holds two BCD digits. That means the legal encodings are not the full 0–255 range. For `hh`, the legal values are `0x01` through `0x12`, representing 01 through 12. For `mm` and `ss`, the legal values are `0x00` through `0x59`, representing 00 through 59. Values such as `0x0A`, `0x13`, `0x60`, or `0xFF` are not alternate representations; they are illegal outputs and should be treated as failures by the checker.

The boundary behavior is the main thing to pin down. Reset is synchronous, active high, and has priority over enable. On a sampled edge with `reset=1`, the settled outputs must be exactly `pm=0`, `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`. This is true whether `ena` is 0 or 1, and it is true regardless of the prior state. A common mistake is to gate reset by enable; the public spec explicitly forbids that. Because power-up state is unspecified, the checker should not judge the first sampled edge unless that edge is a reset edge. A clean sequence begins with a reset sample and then checks from the following settled state.

When `reset=0` and `ena=0`, the settled outputs must equal the prior settled outputs in every field: `pm`, `hh`, `mm`, and `ss`. No increment and no carry may occur. This row distinguishes an enable-gated counter from a free-running one.

When `reset=0` and `ena=1`, the counter advances one second in BCD. Seconds `00` through `58` increment by one; `59` becomes `00` and carries into minutes. Minutes behave the same way: `00` through `58` increment, `59` becomes `00` and carries into hours. Hours follow the 12-hour sequence `12, 01, 02, ..., 11, 12`. Thus `12` goes to `01`, `01` goes to `02`, and `11` goes to `12`. The `pm` flag flips only on the `11→12` transition. It does not flip on `12→01`. So the AM/PM boundary is tied to the 11-to-12 hour rollover, not to the 12-to-1 wrap.

A short worked example: prior settled state `11:59:59 AM` means `pm=0`, `hh=0x11`, `mm=0x59`, `ss=0x59`. On a sampled edge with `reset=0`, `ena=1`, seconds roll to `00`, minutes roll to `00`, hours go to `12`, and `pm` flips to 1. Expected settled outputs are `pm=1`, `hh=0x12`, `mm=0x00`, `ss=0x00`. Input changes after sampling but before the next edge affect only the next row.

For the final Python checker, the most useful targeted row is a reset-with-`ena=0` case starting from a non-reset prior state, requiring the exact reset outputs on that edge. That directly catches enable-gated reset implementations.
