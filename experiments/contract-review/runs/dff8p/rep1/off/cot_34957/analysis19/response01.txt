The public specification defines an 8-bit register bank of D flip-flops, all triggered on the negative edge of `clk`, with active-high synchronous reset. The reset value is `0x34`, not zero. Inputs are `d` (8 bits) and `reset` (1 bit); output is `q` (8 bits). The protocol seed is 20261120, and sampling occurs after the negative edge has settled. Each row’s values are driven before the sampled edge; optional mid-input changes occur after sampling, before the other edge, and are carried to the start of the next row.

The key timing distinction is between the sampled negative edge and any mid-cycle input changes. At the sampled negative edge, the checker must evaluate the inputs that are present immediately before that edge. If `reset` is high at that moment, the expected `q` becomes `0x34`, regardless of `d`. If `reset` is low at that moment, the expected `q` becomes the value of `d` present immediately before the edge. This is the synchronous capture rule. Reset does not asynchronously force `q` to `0x34` when `reset` rises between edges; it only takes effect when a negative clock edge is sampled with `reset` high.

Mid-input changes are explicitly after sampling. They must not retroactively alter the output captured at the just-sampled edge. Instead, they are carried forward and become candidates for the next sampled negative edge. A checker that updates expected `q` on a mid-cycle change to `d` or `reset` will be wrong. A checker that treats reset as an asynchronous override will also be wrong. A checker that captures `d` when reset should dominate will be wrong.

Power-up is unspecified. The checker must not assume `q` starts at zero, at `0x34`, or at any other value. The reset value `0x34` is a capture value at a negative edge, not a power-on default. Any initial expected value must be established by the stimulus, by an explicit sampled reset, or by a known initialization event in the testbench. Seeding expected `q` with `0x34` before the first sampled reset can produce false passes or false failures.

A short worked example clarifies the boundary. Suppose before a sampled negative edge `q` is `0x12`, `reset` is 0, and `d` is `0x5A`. At the sampled negative edge, expected `q` becomes `0x5A`. After sampling, before the next edge, `reset` rises to 1 and `d` changes to `0xAB`. The output `q` must still be `0x5A`; reset is synchronous, so it does not asynchronously force `q` to `0x34` mid-cycle. At the next sampled negative edge, `reset` is high, so expected `q` becomes `0x34`, not `0xAB`. This example separates three common checker mistakes: updating expected `q` on a mid-cycle input change, treating reset as asynchronous, and capturing `d` when reset should dominate.

For the final Python checker, the useful discipline is to model the sampling boundary explicitly. Track row-start inputs, inputs at the sampled negative edge, post-sampling changes, and carried inputs into the next row separately. Update expected `q` only at the sampled negative edge, using the pre-edge `d` and `reset`. Do not update `q` on a mid change. Do not treat reset as an asynchronous override. Do not assume any particular power-up value for `q`. Every reset-sampled negative edge must produce exactly `0x34`, and every non-reset sampled negative edge must capture the current pre-edge `d` rather than a stale or mid-updated value.
