The public specification defines eight D flip-flops sharing a single clock, `clk`, and a single active-high synchronous reset, `reset`. The data input is an 8-bit bus `d`, and the output is an 8-bit bus `q`. The required reset value is `0x34`, not zero. All state changes are triggered by the negative edge of `clk`. The checker must sample after the negative edge has settled, and each row's values are driven before the sampled edge. Optional mid-input changes occur after sampling and before the other edge, and those changes carry into the start of the next row.

The central timing distinction is between pending inputs and committed state. A change on `d` or `reset` between sampled negative edges does not immediately affect `q`. It only becomes effective if it is present at the next sampled negative edge. Thus, a mid-cycle assertion of `reset` must not force `q` to `0x34` immediately; it is merely a pending reset input for the next sampled edge. Similarly, a mid-cycle change on `d` must not alter `q` until the next sampled negative edge. The checker should therefore keep pending `d` and pending `reset` separate from committed `q`, and evaluate the next state only at the sampled edge.

At a sampled negative edge, the committed next state is exactly `0x34` if `reset` is high at that edge; otherwise it is the pending `d` value. There is no enable, load, or asynchronous clear. No other condition should modify `q`. If `reset` and `d` both change mid-cycle, both remain pending until the next sampled negative edge. If `reset` is high at that edge, reset dominates and `q` becomes `0x34` regardless of `d`.

A related pitfall is assuming a defined power-up state. The public contract does not specify whether the flip-flops power up at zero, at `0x34`, or at any other value. It only defines behavior at sampled negative edges. The checker should avoid asserting `q` before the first reset-sampled edge, and it should not infer an initial value from the reset constant. The value `0x34` is the value loaded on a reset-sampled edge, not necessarily the initial state.

A short worked example clarifies the timing. Suppose committed `q` is `0x34`. After a sampled edge, `d` changes to `0x7A` while `reset` stays low. Committed `q` remains `0x34`; the new `d` is pending. At the next sampled negative edge, if no newer `d` has been driven, `q` becomes `0x7A`. If instead the next row drives `d = 0x11` before that edge, `q` becomes `0x11`. If `reset` is high at that edge, `q` becomes `0x34` regardless of `d`.

For the final Python checker, the robust approach is to maintain pending `d` and pending `reset`, update them on row drives and mid-cycle changes, evaluate only at the sampled negative edge, commit `q` from that evaluation, and defer `q` assertions until after the first reset-sampled edge. This avoids the common failures of asynchronous reset modeling, reset-to-zero assumptions, and premature or delayed sampling.
