# Reset and Power-Up Behavior for the Gshare Predictor

The public spec states only that "Reset is asynchronous active-high." That single sentence carries several obligations that the final Python checker must enforce, and they interact with the two other timing hazards (PHT read-before-write and GHR recovery priority) in ways that a naive implementation will get wrong.

**Asynchronous assertion semantics.** Because reset is asynchronous, the checker must be able to assert `areset` at an arbitrary point in the cycle—between edges, not just at a sampling edge—and observe the effect *without* waiting for a posedge. Concretely, the checker should drive `areset=1` mid-cycle (after one sampled edge, before the next), then sample the outputs before the next posedge. All registered state must already be zero: `predict_taken=0`, `predict_history=0`, and the internal GHR and every PHT entry must be cleared. A synchronous-reset design, or one that only clears state on the posedge, will still show stale outputs at that mid-cycle sample and must fail.

**Reset dominance over pending operations.** The spec's priority rules (training beats prediction for the GHR; prediction reads the pre-edge PHT) describe what happens in a *normal* cycle. Reset overrides all of them. If `areset` is asserted in the same cycle that `train_valid` or `predict_valid` is high, the pending operation must not commit. The checker should drive `areset=1` together with a valid training request that would otherwise flip a PHT counter or recover the GHR, then verify on the following cycle that the PHT entry and GHR show *no trace* of that aborted operation—i.e., they remain at their reset values, not at the values the aborted train would have produced. This is the reset analogue of the "training write must not leak into the same-cycle prediction read" hazard: just as a same-cycle train must not affect the prediction output, a same-cycle reset must not let a train affect post-reset state.

**Power-up.** The spec does not define power-up values beyond reset. The checker should treat reset as the sole initialization mechanism: after `areset` is released, the GHR is `7'b0` and all 128 PHT entries are `2'b00` (the standard "weakly not-taken" reset value implied by a two-bit saturating counter cleared to zero). Any test that relies on a specific power-up state without first asserting reset is out of spec and should not be used to discriminate designs.

**Interaction with the other two hazards.** The reset check should be run in isolation from the collision and recovery checks, because reset masks them. But the checker should also confirm ordering: a mid-cycle reset followed immediately by a normal predict/train cycle must behave as if the predictor had just come out of reset—GHR zero, PHT zero—so the first post-reset prediction uses index `pc[6:0] XOR 0` and reads `2'b00`, predicting not-taken. This catches designs that clear outputs on reset but leave the PHT or GHR partially initialized.

**Worked example (reset dominance).** Suppose the GHR is `7'b1010101` and PHT entry 0 holds `2'b11`. Mid-cycle, assert `areset=1` together with `train_valid=1`, `train_taken=0`, `train_mispredicted=1`, `train_history=7'b0000000`, `train_pc` hashing to entry 0. Before the next posedge, `predict_history` and `predict_taken` must read 0. On the following cycle (reset released), the GHR must be `7'b0` and PHT[0] must be `2'b00`—not the `2'b10` that the aborted saturating decrement would have produced, and not the recovered GHR `7'b0000001`. A design that commits the train on the reset edge fails here.

These reset checks, together with the pre-edge PHT read and the recovery-over-shift priority, are the three most discriminating behaviors for this spec and should anchor the final checker.
