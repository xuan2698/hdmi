The timing contract hinges on distinguishing what is visible *before* a clock edge from what is committed *at* that edge. Inputs (`predict_valid`, `predict_pc`, `train_valid`, `train_taken`, `train_mispredicted`, `train_history`, `train_pc`) are driven before the sampled posedge and are stable across it. Outputs (`predict_taken`, `predict_history`) are combinational functions of the current state and the current inputs, sampled after the edge has settled. The critical consequence: a prediction requested in cycle N must reflect the PHT and GHR as they stood *before* any training write scheduled for cycle N. Training effects appear only in cycle N+1.

This creates the central hazard the checker must probe: a same-cycle collision between `predict_valid` and `train_valid` that hash to the same PHT index. The spec is explicit that the prediction sees the pre-training PHT state, because the training write lands at the next posedge. A naive implementation that muxes the training data into the prediction read path—or that samples `predict_taken` after the edge—will return the post-update MSB instead of the pre-update MSB. Isolated predict-only or train-only tests cannot expose this, since the two views coincide when only one operation is active. The discriminating test drives both signals in one cycle against a known counter state, with `train_taken` chosen to flip the counter's MSB, and asserts `predict_taken` equals the *old* MSB while the next-cycle counter reflects the saturating update.

A second hazard is GHR update priority. On a mispredicted training request, the GHR must be recovered to `((train_history << 1) | train_taken) & 0x7F`, and this recovery must override any prediction-driven shift occurring in the same cycle. A design where the prediction shift wins will pass tests that only exercise misprediction recovery with `predict_valid=0`. The checker should assert that in a cycle with both `predict_valid` and a mispredicted `train_valid`, `predict_history` reflects the pre-edge GHR, and the next-cycle GHR equals the recovered value—not the shifted prediction value. Note also that `predict_history` is an output describing the GHR *used* for the prediction, so it must be the pre-edge value even when training will overwrite the register.

A third hazard is asynchronous active-high reset. Because reset is asynchronous, asserting `areset` mid-cycle must immediately zero outputs and prevent any pending train or predict in that row from mutating state. A synchronous-reset implementation, or one that still commits a training write on the reset edge, will fail this. The checker should drive `areset` between edges and confirm outputs collapse to zero without waiting for a posedge, and that the following cycle's state shows no trace of the aborted operation.

Worked example: suppose PHT[index] holds `2'b10` (predict taken) and GHR is `7'b0000000`. In one cycle, assert `predict_valid` with a PC hashing to that index, and `train_valid` with `train_taken=0`, `train_mispredicted=0`, same index. Expected `predict_taken=1` (old MSB), and next-cycle PHT[index]=`2'b01`. A design leaking the training write returns `predict_taken=0` and fails.

These three checks—pre-edge prediction read under collision, recovery-over-shift priority, and reset dominance—are the most discriminating for this spec and should anchor the final checker.
