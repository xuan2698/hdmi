One likely implementation mistake is to let the prediction path and the training path share too much state in the same cycle, especially around the history register and the PHT read/write ports. The public spec makes the intended timing asymmetric: prediction is a combinational read of the current PHT entry and current history register, while training updates the PHT and history only at the next positive clock edge. A common bug is to compute the prediction using a history value that has already been modified by the same-cycle training request, or to let the prediction’s history shift override the training recovery when both are valid. That violates the rule that training takes precedence because the predicted instruction will be flushed anyway.

A second common mistake is to index the PHT incorrectly during training. The training interface supplies `train_pc` and `train_history`; the PHT index must be `train_pc ^ train_history`, not `train_pc ^ current_history_register` and not `train_pc` alone. This matters because the live history register may differ from `train_history` when the branch being trained is older than the current fetch point. Similarly, the recovered history on a mispredicted training must be derived from the supplied `train_history`, namely `{train_history[5:0], train_taken}`, rather than from the live register or from the prediction-side shift. If the implementation uses the live register, the recovery will be wrong whenever training is not for the youngest branch.

A third mistake is to make the PHT write visible to the same-cycle prediction. The spec explicitly says the prediction sees the pre-edge PHT state; training only changes the table at the next positive clock edge. An implementation that performs a combinational bypass from the training write into the prediction read will produce a different `predict_taken` when prediction and training hit the same PHT entry. The checker should therefore compare `predict_taken` against the old PHT contents, not against the post-training value.

A short worked example: suppose the live history register is 0, and in one cycle `predict_valid=1`, `predict_pc=7'b0000001` (index 1), while `train_valid=1`, `train_mispredicted=1`, `train_taken=1`, `train_history=7'b1000000` (64), `train_pc=7'b1000000` (index 64). The prediction must read PHT[1] and output `predict_history=0`. The training must write PHT[64], not PHT[0] or PHT[1], and at the edge must set history to `{train_history[5:0], train_taken} = 7'b0000001`, not to a shift of the live 0 and not to the prediction-shift result. On the next cycle, a prediction at `predict_pc=7'b1000001` (index 65) should see `predict_history=1` and read PHT[65]. This example discriminates against using live history for training indexing, against prediction winning the history update, against recovering from the live register, and against same-cycle PHT bypass.

For the final checker, the highest-value assertions are: sample `predict_taken` and `predict_history` from pre-edge state; verify the PHT write address is `train_pc ^ train_history`; verify mispredicted-training recovery is `{train_history[5:0], train_taken}`; and confirm training overrides prediction for the history register when both are valid. Also check two-bit counter saturation at 0 and 3 and the MSB-as-direction rule at the 1↔2 boundary.
